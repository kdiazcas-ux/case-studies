Case Study

When Nobody Speaks the Same Language

Designing a unified platform for cross-functional teams who had never agreed on anything — until now.

Client Enterprise SaaS
Role Lead UX Designer
Timeline 14 Weeks
Team 5 Members
47% reduction in onboarding time
3x faster cross-team alignment
89% user satisfaction score
01

A Room Full of Experts

The project started with a familiar scene: a conference room full of brilliant people who couldn't agree on what problem they were solving. Engineers talked about technical debt. Product managers worried about roadmap alignment. Designers wanted to focus on user pain points. Leadership just wanted results.

Everyone had a valid perspective, but no shared vocabulary. The first two weeks were spent in meetings that ended with more questions than answers. It became clear that the real challenge wasn't designing an interface — it was designing a way for these teams to think together.

Early whiteboard sessions revealed the depth of misalignment across teams
02

Research First, Design Second

Before opening any design tool, we spent three weeks embedded with each team. We conducted 28 interviews across engineering, product, sales, and customer success. We shadowed daily standups. We read through six months of Slack threads to understand how decisions actually got made.

The research revealed something unexpected: the biggest friction wasn't between departments — it was within them. Senior engineers disagreed with junior engineers about priorities. Product managers had different mental models depending on which customers they'd spoken to most recently. The platform didn't just need to bridge teams; it needed to bridge worldviews.

Affinity mapping sessions helped surface hidden patterns in team communication
03

Listening Before Solving

The impulse to jump into wireframes was strong, but premature solutions would have been built on assumptions. Instead, we created a shared language document — a living glossary that defined terms each team used differently. "Sprint" meant something different to engineering than it did to marketing. "Launch" had three different definitions depending on who you asked.

This exercise alone changed the dynamic. People started referencing the glossary in meetings. They began prefacing statements with "when I say X, I mean…" The act of naming the problem made it smaller.

The shared glossary became the project's most referenced artifact
04

Refining the Invisible Pieces

With alignment improving, we shifted to information architecture. The existing platform had grown organically over four years — features were bolted on without a unifying structure. Navigation reflected the org chart rather than user workflows. Finding anything required knowing which team owned it.

We mapped every feature to actual user tasks rather than departmental ownership. This meant some beloved features got demoted while overlooked tools got elevated. The architecture was designed around what people needed to do, not who built it.

Before and after: reorganizing features by user tasks instead of team ownership
05

The Presentation That Changed the Room

Week seven brought the critical stakeholder review. Rather than presenting polished mockups, we presented the research findings alongside rough concepts. We showed direct quotes from interviews — engineers hearing what sales teams struggled with, and vice versa. We played audio clips of users describing their frustrations.

The room went quiet. For the first time, people were hearing each other's reality. The VP of Engineering turned to the Head of Product and said, "I had no idea your team dealt with that." That moment created more buy-in than any pixel-perfect prototype ever could.

Stakeholder review session — research-led presentations shifted the conversation
06

From Wireframes to Prototypes

With genuine alignment achieved, the design work accelerated. We moved from low-fidelity wireframes to interactive prototypes in two weeks. The shared understanding we'd built meant fewer revision cycles — stakeholders weren't debating fundamentals anymore, they were refining details.

The prototype included three key innovations: a unified dashboard that surfaced cross-team dependencies, a contextual navigation system that adapted based on the user's role and current task, and an activity feed that translated technical updates into language each team could understand.

Interactive prototype evolution from low-fidelity wireframes to high-fidelity screens
07

Where Big Ideas Got Small

Not every idea survived contact with reality. The AI-powered suggestion engine we'd proposed was too ambitious for the technical infrastructure. The real-time collaboration feature introduced performance issues that would have degraded the experience for everyone. Three features we'd championed in early concepts were cut entirely.

But the cuts weren't defeats — they were design decisions. Each removal was intentional, backed by data, and communicated transparently. The product got stronger as it got simpler. We learned that the courage to remove is as important as the creativity to add.

Feature prioritization matrix — what we kept, what we cut, and why
08

From Understanding to Architecture

The final design system reflected everything we'd learned. Components were named using the shared vocabulary we'd established in week three. Design tokens mapped to the roles and workflows we'd documented. The system wasn't just a UI kit — it was an embodiment of the organizational alignment we'd facilitated.

We documented every decision, every trade-off, every cut. The design system included not just components and patterns, but the reasoning behind them. Future designers and developers could understand not just what to build, but why it was built that way.

Design system components built on shared vocabulary and documented decisions
09

What Changed

Six months after launch, the numbers told a clear story. Onboarding time dropped by 47%. Cross-team ticket resolution went from an average of 12 days to 4. The quarterly satisfaction survey showed an 89% approval rating — up from 51% the year before.

But the most meaningful change wasn't quantitative. Teams started inviting each other to planning sessions. Engineers began attending user research calls. The shared language we'd introduced became part of the company culture. The platform wasn't just a tool anymore — it was a bridge.

Impact metrics six months post-launch
10

What I Learned

This project reinforced something I keep rediscovering: design is mostly not about screens. The most impactful work happened in conversations — the interviews that revealed hidden pain, the glossary that gave people a shared vocabulary, the presentation that created empathy between teams.

The pixel work mattered, of course. But the pixels were only as good as the understanding behind them. If I'd started with wireframes in week one, I would have built a beautiful product that solved the wrong problem. The patience to listen first made everything that followed possible.

"The best interfaces aren't the ones users learn — they're the ones that already speak the user's language."
